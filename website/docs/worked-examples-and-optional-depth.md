# Worked Examples and Optional Depth

Use these cards after the corresponding core teaching. They supply the scenario and a sample response; no external research, new account, real-world contact, or private disclosure is required. The examples are fictional teaching cases, not real measurements, medical guidance, legal rulings, financial advice, or promises of outcomes.

## Two kinds of optional work

- **Supplied practice:** the scenario, sample response, and depth question are on this page. Allow about 15–20 minutes for one card, with paper or oral explanation. Read the card before the session; a calculator is optional for arithmetic.
- **Open research prompt:** a request to locate or construct missing teaching material, such as outside sources, interview records, or a real-case packet. This is not supplied instruction. A tool activity with complete steps is still supplied instruction, even when an adult must prepare the device or account. An adult must select and verify materials, arrange access, and plan additional time first. Use the supplied card instead when those materials are unavailable.

Choose depth by readiness and interest, not age alone. Learners aged 10–12 can use the core teaching without taking every technical add-on; older-learner work remains optional. The depth questions are not prerequisites for the next core week or criteria for its checkpoint.

## How to use a card

1. Read the scenario and ask for a reasoned answer.
2. Let the learner draw, speak, sort, or write using the fictional case.
3. Compare with the sample: notice the evidence and the limits, not matching wording.
4. If ready, change the stated assumption using the depth question.
5. If the core idea is unclear, return to the linked lesson before adding complexity. A private or paper artifact is enough.

Sample responses illustrate reasoning, not scripts to memorize or the only defensible answer to an open choice. They do not require a learner to experience or disclose the situation.

## Week 1
**Core lesson:** [Week 1: Mapping Your Biological Hardware](./week01-internal-telemetry.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional character notices tight shoulders before a loud assembly. What can the observation tell us?

**Illustrative response:** It is a body signal worth noticing, not a diagnosis or proof of one emotion. The character can pause or ask a safe adult for support.

**Optional depth:** Give two possible interpretations and say what remains unknown. Personal body reports are not required.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 2

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional character slept poorly and finds a busy room harder to manage. What is a careful explanation?

**Optional depth:** Name one accessible support the adult could provide without blaming the learner for limited capacity.

## Week 3
**Core lesson:** [Week 3: The Brain's Emergency Override](./week03-the-amygdala-switch.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional fire alarm makes a character freeze. Is freezing a choice to ignore instructions?

**Illustrative response:** Not necessarily. Fast protective responses can interrupt planning. Adults should support safety; a calm voice or perfect action is not required to deserve help.

**Optional depth:** Describe an adult support route rather than asking the character to prove they can control the response.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 4
**Core lesson:** [Week 4: Reading Your Own Early Warning Signs](./week04-the-telemetry-log.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Use an invented log: loud room, tense hands, request for quiet space. What belongs in a sample entry?

**Illustrative response:** Record the situation, observed signal, possible interpretation, and support step. Keep certainty limited; a private learner journal is not evidence the adult must inspect.

**Optional depth:** Separate one observation from one inference and choose another explanation to check.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 5
**Core lesson:** [Week 5: The Software Bugs of the Mind](./week05-cognitive-distortions.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional thought says "I made one mistake, so I fail at everything." What is the thought pattern?

**Illustrative response:** It turns one event into an all-or-nothing generalization. A more accurate statement is "I made a mistake on this task and can ask what to try next."

**Optional depth:** Keep the real disappointment valid while reducing the unsupported generalization.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 8
**Core lesson:** [Week 8: Separating Signal from Narrative Noise](./week08-the-signal-audit.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional note says "Three people laughed after I dropped a pencil; everyone thinks I’m useless." Audit it.

**Illustrative response:** Separate the observed laughter from the broad conclusion. We do not know the reason or what everyone thinks. If teasing or harm is involved, seek adult support.

**Optional depth:** Choose a safe verification or support step; checking evidence is not permission to dismiss mistreatment.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

**Optional depth:** Name a safety limit: a protocol is not a plan for tolerating threats or forcing private disclosure.
